Interactive Network Visualization with D3

For this lab, I first implemented the two visualizations, the Node Link Visualization and the Adjacency Matrix, respectively, followed by relevant descriptions and my answers to the questions. Enjoy!

1. Dataset Description

View 1

2. Node Link Visualization

Feel free to drag any station to rearrange the network visualization. You may hover over or focus a station to highlight its direct neighbors, and you can also inspect the stations and routes through their tooltips. Enjoy!

3. Node-Link Design Description

Notice that in this node-link view, I use color (grey, red, green, blue, and yellow) to identify the district, and I use the area of the circle node to represent the daily passengers on a common scale, with larger circle standing for more passengers. The filling of each node distinguishes the roles of that station: solid for Terminal, half-filled for Transfer, and outlined for Local. Solid, dashed, and dotted edges represent Express, Metro, and Shuttle respectively, while the width of that edge represents travel time, the wider the width, the longer the travel time. Also notice that dragging separates overlaps, highlighting isolates neighbors, and tooltips give exact values.

View 2

4. Adjacency Matrix

For the Adjacency Matrix, we use the rows and columns to represent the same district-grouped station order. Notice that each colored cell marks a direct connection. Color denotes route type, while density denotes travel time. Enjoy!

5. Matrix Design Description

Notice that the adjacency matrix places the same district-grouped order on both axes, so a filled cell identifies a direct connection. The coloring red, green, and blue denote Express, Metro, and Shuttle respectively, with three density levels classifying travel time, grouped into three categories. I also use pink axis strips to denote district memberships. Finally, this grouping reveals within- and cross-district patterns.

6. Network Questions and Findings

  1. Which stations appear central in the network?

    This can be partially answered with the Node-Link View. The hover highlighting exposes each immediate neighborhood to that node. Fourteen stations share the maximum degree of three, so the visualization does not identify one uniquely central hub.

  2. Which districts are strongly connected to one another?

    This can be answered well by the Adjacency Matrix. The grouped label strips and filled cells reveal 11 East–South connections, compared with nine Central–North and nine East–West connections, so I think these three (E-S, C-N, E-W) are the districts that are strongly connected.

  3. Where are transfer or terminal stations located in the topology?

    This can be answered well by the Node-Link view. The half-filled and solid circles show Transfer and Terminal stations distributed along the main chain and at shortcut endpoints rather than concentrated in one cluster. Terminals are Stations 1, 4, 7, …, 49, while Transfer stations are 2, 5, 8, …, 50.

  4. Which stations have high passenger volume?

    This can be answered well by the Node-Link view. The circle area makes Stations 46–50 visibly largest, and Station 50 is the leading station with 9,850 daily passengers, followed by Station 49 (9677) and Station 48 (9504).

  5. Where are the longest direct travel-time connections?

    This can be answered well by both views. The widest edges in Node Link View and darkest matrix cells in Adjacency Matrix identify Stations 7–31 at 16 minutes, 18–34 at 15 minutes, and 29–50 at 14 minutes. These are the longest ones.

  6. Are particular route types concentrated in particular parts of the network?

    This can be answered partially by the Adjacency Matrix. The cell colors show broad dispersal: Metro and Shuttle each have four East–South links, while Express has four links in both Central–North and East–West.
