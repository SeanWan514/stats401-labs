STATS 401 · Lab 4

Cleaning Web Data for Visualization

A reproducible Python pipeline cleans 1,500 tweets, estimates sentiment with RoBERTa, and prepares tidy data for D3.

Dataset Davidson et al. hate-speech and offensive-language corpus SourceMIT-licensed GitHub repository Records analyzed 1,500 tweets Sentiment model CardiffNLP Twitter RoBERTa

Method and Limitation

The source provides raw tweet text and human annotations for hate speech, offensive language, or neither. Those annotations are used only as the comparison attribute. Sentiment is recalculated from every tweet with cardiffnlp/twitter-roberta-base-sentiment-latest. The probabilities and labels are model-generated estimates, not ground truth; sarcasm, slang, reclaimed language, and limited context can produce errors.

D3 Visualization

RoBERTa Sentiment by Annotation Category

Each bar totals 100%. Move your pointer over a segment, or focus it with the keyboard, to inspect its count, share, and average sentiment score.

Sentiment score equals P(positive) minus P(negative), ranging approximately from −1 to 1.

Short Analysis

Analysis

This analysis uses a deterministic 1,500-tweet sample from the MIT-licensed Davidson et al. hate-speech and offensive-language corpus. The Python pipeline checked for missing and duplicate records, converted annotation counts to numeric values, verified that annotation votes were internally consistent, normalized whitespace, and created separate text representations for TF-IDF and sentiment analysis. Sentiment was estimated for every retained tweet with the CardiffNLP Twitter RoBERTa model. The sentiment score is the predicted positive probability minus the predicted negative probability. In the 100% stacked bars, position shows the share assigned to each sentiment while color distinguishes negative, neutral, and positive predictions; the second attribute is the original human annotation category. Negative predictions dominate the hate-speech group (88.0%) and the offensive-language group (71.3%). The neither category is more balanced: 39.3% negative, 39.7% neutral, and 21.0% positive. These differences suggest that RoBERTa detects strongly negative language in the two harmful-language categories, although its outputs remain model estimates and should not be treated as ground truth.
